  - The teacher-student dialogue should deepen SOS, emergency signals, winter

  - Vocabulary and grammar sections should explain language tools, not retell
    the whole story again.
- Generated illustrations should be almost text-free.
  - The only permitted readable text inside generated images is the essential

  - No other readable English or Chinese text inside images: no signs, captions,

  - Render words such as `signal`, `stamp`, `helicopter`, `survivor`,

- Keep script and image prompts separate.
  - Teaching script must not contain image prompt wording, camera directions,

## Lesson Positioning

Lesson 75 is an emergency-survival story built around a very simple but powerful
signal. A light passenger plane flies off course and crashes in snowy mountains.
The pilot is killed, but the only passengers, a young woman and her two baby
daughters, are unhurt. They are far from the nearest village, it is the middle
of winter, and snow lies thick on the ground. As night falls, the woman turns a
suitcase into a bed, puts the children inside, covers them with all the clothes
she can find, and stays as near to them as possible. The next morning, she hears
planes overhead and realizes she needs to send a signal. She stamps out `SOS`
in the snow. A pilot sees the signal, radios the nearest town, and soon a
helicopter arrives to rescue the survivors.

The courseware should make the student understand:

`飞机偏离航线 -> 在雪山坠毁 -> 妈妈和两个女婴没有受伤 -> 冬天、雪很厚、村庄很远 -> 天黑了，妈妈把手提箱变成小床 -> 她把所有衣服盖在孩子身上保暖 -> 夜里很冷，她尽量贴近孩子 -> 早晨听到飞机从头顶飞过 -> 她想到要发一个别人能看见的信号 -> 在雪地里踩出巨大的 SOS -> 飞行员看见信号，用无线电报信 -> 直升飞机赶来救出幸存者`

The central bridge is:

`现代孩子习惯了手机、定位和网络，但这篇课文提醒我们：紧急情况下，最重要的是先冷静下来，利用身边的东西保护自己，再用别人能看懂、能看见的方式求救。`

## One-Sentence Hook

When a phone cannot help you, a clear signal in the snow can become the bridge
between danger and rescue.

The opening should set up emergency signals and calm thinking quickly. It must
be a short hook, not a complete retelling.

Suggested teacher narration:

`如果在山里遇到危险，手机没有信号，你会怎么办？`

`这课里，一位妈妈没有电话、没有导航，身边只有雪、衣服和一个手提箱。`

`她先让孩子暖和安全，再想办法让天上的飞机看见她。`

`她踩出的三个字母很短：SOS。`

`今天我们学的不是惊险，而是冷静、求救信号和英文里非常有画面感的动作表达。`

Tone:

- Calm, suspenseful, and hopeful.
- Keep the mother capable and steady.
- Avoid sensational disaster language.
- Save the full sequence for the four `retell` frames.

## Section Responsibility And Anti-Repetition Plan

### Opening

Job:

- Introduce the problem of emergency communication without phone or network.
- Introduce `SOS` as a recognizable distress signal.
- Frame the mother as calm and resourceful.

Do not:

- Retell every rescue step.
- Make the crash frightening or graphic.
- Turn the opening into a safety lecture.

### Four Retell Frames

Job:

- Walk through the passage in order.
- Make the survival sequence easy to remember.
- Use Chinese anchors and short English chunks.

Do not:

- Put readable text inside images except the snow signal `SOS`.
- Show gore, flames, dead bodies, distressed babies, or action-movie danger.
- Make each frame a separate unrelated illustration; the same woman, babies,
  suitcase, snowy mountain setting, and rescue chain should stay continuous.

### Teacher-Student Dialogue

Job:

- Deepen five ideas:
  1. what `SOS` is and why people can recognize it
  2. why a phone or GPS may be unavailable in a remote mountain emergency
  3. why warmth and shelter come before clever solutions at night
  4. why a huge ground signal can be seen from the air
  5. how the rescue chain works: signal -> pilot -> radio -> town -> helicopter
- Keep the discussion practical and child-understandable.
- Connect to safe modern examples: hiking, outdoor camp, snow day, or losing
  the path in a park with no signal.

Do not:

- Repeat the full four-frame retell script.
- Overteach survival medicine or technical aviation details.
- Encourage risky imitation or dramatic role-play.

### Vocabulary And Grammar

Job:

  `case`, `overhead`, `signal`, `stamp out`, `by radio`, `helicopter`,
  `scene`, `rescue`, `survivor`.
- Teach object transformation:
  `turn A into B` in `turned a suitcase into a bed`.
- Teach useful survival-description language:
  `all the clothes she could find`, `as near as she could`.
- Teach timing and rescue pace:
  `It was not long before...`

Do not:

- Turn the vocabulary page into a plane-crash glossary.

### Practice And Living-Scene Transfer

## Cultural And Background Bridge

The lesson needs a compact modern bridge:

1. SOS
   - `SOS` is widely recognized as a distress signal.
   - The point for this lesson is not the origin story of the letters.
   - The point is that it is short, clear, visible, and understood by rescuers.
2. Emergency signals without a phone
   - Modern children may assume help always comes through a phone.
   - In mountains, snow, storms, or remote places, phones can lose signal or
     battery.
   - A visible signal, a bright object, or large letters on the ground can still
     help.
3. Rescue chain
   - The woman does not directly call a helicopter.
   - A pilot sees the signal, sends a radio message, and then a helicopter is
     sent to the scene.
   - This helps the child understand `by radio`, `nearest town`, and
     `arrived on the scene`.
4. Calm survival choices
   - First priority: keep the babies warm and safe through the night.
   - Second priority: send a clear signal when planes pass overhead.
   - The lesson is a good place to teach calm thinking under pressure.

Keep this background short. It should support the story, not become a technical
survival class.

## Teacher-Student Dialogue Direction

The teacher-student dialogue should not replay the whole plot. It should be a
short guided discussion around `SOS` and calm problem-solving.

Suggested dialogue spine:

- Teacher asks why the woman could not simply ask for help in the usual modern
  way.
- Student says maybe there was no phone, no signal, or no people nearby.
- Teacher explains `miles away` and remote mountain isolation.
- Teacher asks what she did first at night.
- Student notices she protected the babies: suitcase bed, clothes, staying
  close.
- Teacher asks why `SOS` in the snow works.
- Student answers that planes can see big letters from the air.
- Teacher explains the rescue chain:
  `signal -> pilot -> radio -> town -> helicopter`.
- Teacher closes with the idea:
  calm thinking can turn ordinary things into rescue tools.

Possible comparison points:

- `signal` versus `message`
- `suitcase` versus `case`
- `rescue` versus `survive`
- `overhead` versus `nearby`

Avoid:

- Too much technical aviation detail.
- Fear-heavy questioning.
- Making the student memorize emergency facts instead of understanding the
  passage.

## Vocabulary Direction

Use exactly 3 formal vocabulary blocks in the generated script.

Recommended formal vocabulary blocks:

1. `signal`
   - Core meaning: something that sends information or asks for attention.
   - Lesson use: `send a signal`, `saw the signal`.
   - Modern bridge: phone signal, hand signal, emergency signal.
2. `stamp`
   - Core meaning: press hard with the foot, or mark something by pressing.
   - Lesson use: `stamped out the letters 'SOS' in the snow`.
   - Focus: physical action plus result.
3. `survivor`
   - Core meaning: a person who remains alive after danger, accident, or

## Grammar Handling

Teach grammar through the story's useful sentence patterns rather than abstract
labels.

Priority structures:

1. Time clauses and sequencing
   - `When a light passenger plane flew off course...`
   - `When it grew dark...`
   - `Early next morning...`
   - Use them to show how English walks the reader through time.
2. `turn A into B`
   - Source sentence:
     `she turned a suitcase into a bed`
   - Teaching point:
     change one thing's use or identity into another.
   - Modern examples:
     `turn a box into a desk`, `turn a scarf into a flag`.
3. Participial phrase for an added action
   - Source phrase:
     `covering them with all the clothes she could find`
   - Teaching point:
     one sentence can show the main action and the supporting action together.
4. `as ... as one can/could`
   - Source sentence:
     `The woman kept as near as she could to the children`
   - Teaching point:
     doing something to the greatest possible degree under the situation.
5. `It was not long before...`
   - Source sentence:
     `It was not long before a helicopter arrived on the scene`
   - Teaching point:
     a concise way to say something happened soon afterward.

Avoid:

- A long grammar lecture on every tense in the story.
- Losing the concrete survival scene while explaining structures.

## Read-Aloud Direction

The read-aloud section should use 5-8 high-value sentences. It should help the
student feel the rhythm of long English sentences and the emotional shift from
danger to rescue.

Recommended guided sentences:

1. `The only passengers, a young woman and her two baby daughters, were unhurt.`
2. `Snow lay thick on the ground.`
3. `When it grew dark, she turned a suitcase into a bed and put the children inside it, covering them with all the clothes she could find.`
4. `The woman kept as near as she could to the children and even tried to get into the case herself, but it was too small.`
5. `Early next morning, she heard planes passing overhead and wondered how she could send a signal.`
6. `She stamped out the letters 'SOS' in the snow.`
7. `Fortunately, a pilot saw the signal and sent a message by radio to the nearest town.`
8. `It was not long before a helicopter arrived on the scene to rescue the survivors of the plane crash.`

Focus:

- Pause after time phrases such as `When it grew dark` and
  `Early next morning`.
- Keep long sentences smooth, not rushed.
- Emphasize key survival verbs: `turned`, `covering`, `kept`, `heard`,
  `stamped`, `saw`, `sent`, `arrived`.
- Do not dramatize the crash sentence in a frightening way.

Practice goals:

- Use `turn A into B`.
- Use `as ... as I could`.

- Keep the scene about calm help-seeking, not fear.

Suggested student-facing sentence frames:

- `We needed to send a signal because _____.`
- `We turned _____ into _____.`
- `I stayed as _____ as I could.`
- `It was not long before _____.`

Do not:

- Ask the student to recreate a plane crash.
- Put the original English passage above fill-in blanks as answers.
